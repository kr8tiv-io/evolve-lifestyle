EVOLVE ← The Journal Snow · November 24, 2026 · 15 min read Minus Thirty and Wide Open: Western Canada's Best Winter Adventures Ten winter days worth planning a season around, from a 30 km skating trail and a frozen canyon to a castle made of snow, in the order they happen. There is a sound a frozen lake makes on a still night. It is not quite a crack and not quite a song. It is a long, low twang that rolls under your boots and keeps going toward the far shore, like someone plucked a steel cable the length of the valley. People who hear it for the first time usually stop walking. People who grew up here usually smile, because it means the ice is thickening and the season has properly started. That sound is the starting gun for this list. We have already written about the places that look better under snow , the hot springs worth the drive , and where to stand for the northern lights . This one is different. It is a calendar. Ten real days and nights across British Columbia, Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, the Yukon, and the Northwest Territories, in the order they happen between the first good ice in December and the last snow castle in March. Some are festivals with music and fire. Some are frozen canyons and lakes you can walk or skate for kilometres. All of them are reasons to be outside on purpose when most of the continent is hiding indoors. Every date below was checked against the organizer's own page in late November 2026. Winter writes its own schedule, though, so check again before you drive. How to use this calendar The list runs in date order, not in order of preference. Pick one from each month and you have a whole season. Pick the four that land on the same week in February and you have a road trip (we mapped one out near the end). Two of the ten depend on ice rather than a printed date: the Grotto Canyon icewalk and the Lake Windermere Whiteway. Those open when nature says so. The other eight are festivals with fixed 2027 dates. December: the ice comes in 1. Grotto Canyon icewalk, Bow Valley, Alberta When: Through the cold months, once the creek bed freezes. Better later in the season, when the falls have had time to build. Where: Grotto Mountain day-use area off Highway 1A, about ten minutes from Canmore. Most people drive past Grotto Canyon on the way to somewhere famous. That is their loss. A short walk from the parking lot, the trail drops into a creek bed that narrows into a limestone slot, the walls climbing higher with every bend, until you are walking up a frozen stream between two grey cliffs that block out most of the sky. Where the canyon forks, go right. At the end is a frozen waterfall, a hanging column of blue and white ice that looks like it stopped mid-sentence. Look for the quieter treasure on the way. Just before the falls, on the left at about eye level, the smooth rock carries faint pictographs that Travel Alberta dates at 500 to 1,000 years old. They are a protected historic site. Look closely, photograph them if you like, and do not touch them. The oils on your hands do more damage than a century of weather. Travel Alberta lists the walk at 4.2 km return to the waterfall with about 170 m of elevation. That is easy on paper. On ice, it is a different walk. Microspikes are the single best piece of gear you can bring. Good to know: Parking here needs a Kananaskis Conservation Pass. Alberta.ca lists the personal vehicle day pass at $15 and the yearly pass at $90 (checked 24 Nov 2026). Buy it before you leave town, and check the Alberta Parks trail reports for the Bow Valley before you go. 2. The Lake Windermere Whiteway, Invermere, British Columbia When: Once the lake ice passes the grooming club's thickness policy, usually in the heart of winter. There is no fixed opening day. Where: Access points at Kinsmen Beach in Invermere and at Windermere Beach. Picture a skating rink about 30 km long, laid down on a mountain lake between the Rockies and the Purcells. That is the Whiteway. The Toby Creek Nordic Ski Club built it in 2006 to connect the communities around Lake Windermere through the winter, and it now grooms the loop for skating, classic and skate skiing, fat biking, and walking. Guinness World Records measured it at 29.98 km in 2014, and the club says it has been the world's longest skating track since. The magic of the Whiteway is how ordinary it makes something extraordinary. Families skate it with a thermos. Skiers push past them in long, quiet strides. Somebody always has a dog. On a blue morning the whole valley seems to be out on the ice, and the only sounds are blades, poles, and that long low twang from under the surface. Good to know: The club sells day and season passes, publishes an ice thickness policy, and posts real-time grooming and condition updates on its social channels. Check those before you load the car. Never step onto ungroomed or unmarked ice to cut a corner. January: the festivals wake up 3. Deep Freeze: A Byzantine Winter Festival, Edmonton, Alberta When: January 16 and 17, 2027. Saturday runs 12 PM to 9 PM, with fireworks at 9 PM. Sunday runs 12 PM to 6 PM. Where: 118 Avenue between 90 and 95 Street, in the Alberta Avenue district. Cost: Free. Deep Freeze celebrates the Olde New Year, and 2027 is its 20th anniversary, with the theme "Discover Boreal." It is the first festival on Edmonton's calendar, and it has the most character per city block of anything on this list. Ukrainian, French Canadian, Franco-African, Indigenous, Latin American, and Asian traditions share one stretch of avenue. You can snowshoe and roast bannock over an open fire in Pipon Village, then wander back out to watch the Deep Freezer Races, which are exactly what they sound like: teams racing old chest freezers down the street. It is the best possible argument that a prairie city in January is not something to endure. It is something to throw a party for. Good to know: Park legally. The festival warns that the City tickets and tows illegally parked vehicles around the site. 4. Rossland Winter Carnival, Rossland, British Columbia When: January 28 to 31, 2027. Where: Downtown Rossland, in the West Kootenay. Tourism Rossland calls it Canada's longest-running winter festival. It started in 1898, when a Norwegian miner named Olaus Jeldness decided a mountain mining town needed a reason to celebrate the snow. More than a century later, the town still agrees with him. The headline event is the Sonny Samuelson Bobsled Race. Teams of four design and build their own sleds, then ride them down eight icy blocks of Spokane Street, one of the steepest streets in town. Tourism Rossland says the sleds reach 70 km/h or more. The crowd lines the street and cheers every spin. Around that, there is the Olaus Ice Palace, an outdoor venue built from snow, the Blizzard Music Festival in the downtown venues after dark, and maple taffy in between. This is a small mountain town, and the beds go fast. Book early, and bring a reason to stay an extra day. The snowy trails around town are made for fat bikes, skis, and snowshoes. 5. Chinook Blast, Calgary, Alberta When: January 29 to February 15, 2027 (the festival's own announcement). Some tourism listings show February 16, so confirm the final day before planning around it. Where: Eau Claire Plaza and the Prince's Island Park lagoon, downtown Calgary. Cost: The entertainment is free. Calgary named its signature winter festival after the warm wind that pours off the Rockies and can turn a frozen morning into a mild afternoon. Chinook Blast spreads glowing art, music, cultural showcases, and local markets across three weekends on the river at Eau Claire. Two curated market weekends run at the Calgary Chinese Cultural Centre next door, January 29 to 31 and February 12 to 14. The full schedule lands in January.
