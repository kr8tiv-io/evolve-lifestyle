Everything you need to go from never drilled a hole to a good first day on the ice. Every winter, somebody you know disappears onto a frozen lake on a Saturday morning and comes back with a story, a sunburn on the one strip of face they forgot, and maybe a limit of trout. They make it sound easy. It mostly is. Ice fishing is one of the cheapest, friendliest ways to spend a winter day outside in Alberta. You sit on a bucket, you jig a little, you drink something hot, and the fish come to you. It's also the easiest way we know to get into real trouble in a hurry. Thin ice doesn't care that the guy on the next lake was out last weekend. A propane heater in a closed tent can make you sick without you noticing. And a fine for the wrong licence will cost you more than the gear. So this is the guide we wish someone had handed us before our first hole. It's in the order you need it: the licence, the ice, the kit, what to wear, where to go first, what you caught, and how to cook it. We checked the numbers against the 2026 Alberta Guide to Sportfishing Regulations, My Wild Alberta, the Lifesaving Society, the Canadian Red Cross, Health Canada and the province's 2026 fish stocking report in November 2026. Rules change by lake and by season, so check again the week you go. Step one: the licence (it's cheaper than lunch) Alberta keeps this simple. Before you buy any fishing licence, you need a Wildlife Identification Number (WiN) . It's a one-time activation. The 2026 regulations list the fee at $12 plus GST, with $2 off when you do it online, so most people pay $10 plus GST at AlbertaRELM . After that, your WiN doesn't expire. Then the licence itself. Fees below are from My Wild Alberta and the 2026 regulations, before the 5% GST: Who you are Annual 7-day 1-day Alberta resident, 16 to 64 $30 Not offered Not offered Alberta resident, under 16 or 65 and older No licence needed No licence needed No licence needed Canadian from outside Alberta, 16 and older $62 $43 $27 Visitor from outside Canada, 16 and older $87 $57 $29 Canadian Armed Forces veterans who live in Alberta can get a sportfishing licence at no charge. Kids under 16 and Alberta residents 65 and older don't need a licence, but they still have to follow every rule in the regulations. You can buy online at AlbertaRELM or from more than 300 licence issuers around the province, which in practice means most tackle shops and a lot of hardware stores. Want to try it first for free? Alberta runs Family Fishing Weekends twice a year, when anyone can fish waters with an open season without buying a sportfishing licence. The next winter one is February 13 to 15, 2027 , over the Family Day long weekend. The regulations still apply, a licence is still needed if you're using a walleye Special Harvest Licence, and free fishing doesn't cover the national parks. Details are on My Wild Alberta's Family Fishing Weekends page . Step two: the rules that catch beginners The full regulation guide is long, and most of it is lake-by-lake tables. You don't need to memorize it. You need to know a handful of province-wide rules, and you need to look up the one lake you're going to. Here are the ones that matter most on the ice, straight from the 2026 general regulations : Two lines, maximum, when you're fishing through ice. One line in open water. Stay within 30 m of any line you have in the water. No drilling six holes and wandering off to visit. No more than three hooks on a line, and no hook with more than three points. No live fish as bait, and no frogs or salamanders. Dead bait and other legal baits are fine where bait is allowed. No chumming. Bait has to be on a hook, not scattered down the hole. Don't dump leftover bait within 50 m of the water unless it goes in a regularly serviced garbage bin. Never move a live fish to a different lake or pond. It's illegal and it's how lakes get wrecked. Then check your specific lake. The free Alberta Sportfishing Regulations app and the printed guide list each waterbody's season, bait rules and limits. Some lakes are catch-and-release only for certain species. Some close for part of the year. On some walleye lakes, you can only keep a walleye with a Special Harvest Licence tag for that lake. If you only remember one habit, make it this: look up the lake before you load the truck. The good news for beginners: most stocked trout ponds in Alberta are managed as "put-and-take" fisheries. Under the provincial put-and-take regulations they're open all year, bait is allowed, and you can keep 5 trout of any size , plus 3 northern pike and 15 yellow perch if those are present. That's why every lake in our starter list below is a stocked pond. Fewer special rules, more fish, and usually a parking lot. Step three: the ice, or you stay in the truck Here's the part we're not going to be funny about. There's no such thing as safe ice. There's only ice that has been measured, in more than one place, by someone who knows what the numbers mean. The Lifesaving Society's recommended minimums for new, clear, hard ice are the ones most Albertans use: Clear, hard ice Good for 7 cm (3 in.) or less Stay off 10 cm (4 in.) Walking, ice fishing, cross-country skiing 13 to 18 cm (5 to 7 in.) One snowmobile or ATV 20 to 30 cm (8 to 12 in.) One car, or a group of people 30 to 38 cm (12 to 15 in.) One medium truck (pickup or van) The province's own line in the 2026 regulations is similar: never walk on ice less than 10 cm thick and don't drive on ice less than 30 cm thick. The Canadian Red Cross is more cautious, with its 15-20-25 rule : 15 cm for one person walking or skating, 20 cm for a group, and 25 cm for snowmobiles and ATVs. For a first season, we'd follow the Red Cross numbers. You lose nothing by waiting another week. Those numbers only count for clear, hard ice. The Lifesaving Society says white ice has air or snow inside it and should be treated as suspect. Grey, slushy ice means water. Ice that thawed and refroze is weaker than it looks, and spring ice is rotten ice, even when it measures thick. Snow on top acts like a blanket and slows freezing, so the ice under a fresh dump can be thinner than the ice beside it. Moving water is the big one: inlets, outlets, springs, river mouths and anything near a current. Aerated lakes have their own danger. Many stocked lakes in Alberta run aerators all winter to keep the trout alive. Aerators create open water and thin ice, sometimes far from where they were last year. These lakes are posted with signs that say "DANGER: unpredictable thin ice and open water." Believe the sign. Stay outside fences, give the open water a wide berth, and don't fish near the aerator lines. Two of our starter lakes, Hasse and Winchell, are on the Alberta Conservation Association's aerated lakes list . How to check it yourself: Go with a buddy. The Lifesaving Society says half of ice-related deaths happened when the person was alone. Tell someone where you're going and when you'll be back. Walk out slowly and strike the ice ahead of you with a spud bar (a long steel ice chisel). If it goes through in one or two hits, turn around on your own tracks. Drill test holes as you go and measure the clear ice with a tape. Do it again every few metres. Ice thickness can change in a few steps. Wear ice picks around your neck, carry a throw rope, and consider a flotation suit, or a lifejacket over your winter layers. Stay off the ice at night and stay sober. The Lifesaving Society links alcohol to about 1 in 3 ice-related deaths. If you go through. The Lifesaving Society's advice is short, and worth reading twice. Don't panic.
