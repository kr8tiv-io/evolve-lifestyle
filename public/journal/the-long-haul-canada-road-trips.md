EVOLVE ← The Journal Routes · October 27, 2026 · 12 min read The Long Haul: Canada's Greatest Road Trips, With the Gas Stations Nobody Warns You About From the 232 km winter gap on the Icefields Parkway to 369 km of gravel on the Dempster and 402 km on the Trans-Labrador, here is the real fuel math on Canada's longest drives. Every glossy travel feature about driving across Canada uses the same photograph. There is a clean pickup or a rented campervan parked at an empty viewpoint, morning light hitting a glacier, and a caption about freedom. Nobody in those photographs is looking at the dashboard, doing mental division between remaining range and a headwind out of the west, and realizing that the little gas pump icon on their phone map belongs to a lodge that boarded up its windows after Thanksgiving. In most of North America, an empty highway means you might have to settle for bad coffee at the next exit in twenty minutes. On Canada's serious northern and mountain corridors, an empty highway means the next open fuel pump is 232 to 402 kilometres down the road, cell service died an hour ago behind a limestone ridge, and the only thing standing near the shoulder is a 900-kilogram wood bison licking road salt off the centre line. That is not a reason to stay home. Those stretches, where the radio gives up, the traffic thins to logging trucks and locals who wave with two fingers off the steering wheel, and the valleys open up to country that has not changed much since the last ice sheet pulled back, are the entire point of owning a vehicle with four wheels and a heater. You just need the real math before you pull out of town with three-eighths of a tank and a warm sense of optimism. Here is the field guide to six of Canada's greatest long-haul drives, with the verified kilometre gaps between working pumps, the October and winter closures that catch people every single year, the cell dead zones, and what belongs in the back of the truck before the pavement turns to shale. Quick-reference: Canada's longest highway fuel gaps Before you trust a mapping app that thinks a summer fishing camp with a gravity tank is a 24-hour highway cardlock, here is how the six corridors stack up on the numbers that actually matter. 1. The Icefields Parkway (Alberta Highway 93 North, Lake Louise to Jasper): 232 km total distance. Longest summer gap is 153 km (Saskatchewan River Crossing to Jasper); longest off-season gap from mid-October to mid-April is the full 232 km direct from Lake Louise to Jasper because The Crossing Resort closes completely for winter. Zero cell service for roughly 200 km; paved; winter tires or chains mandatory November 1 to March 31. 2. The Alaska Highway (BC Highway 97 and Yukon Highway 1, Dawson Creek to Whitehorse): roughly 1,420 km to Whitehorse (2,232 km to Delta Junction, Alaska). Longest summer gap is 176 km (Buckinghorse River to Fort Nelson) and 170 km (Burwash Landing to Beaver Creek); longest off-season gap is 206 to 275 km between Muncho Lake and Contact Creek or Watson Lake because Coal River Lodge closes September through May, Liard River Lodge has no retail gas, and rural lodges do not run 24-hour pumps. 3. The Stewart-Cassiar Highway (BC Highway 37, Kitwanga to Junction 37 and Watson Lake): 724 km to Junction 37 (747 km into Watson Lake). Longest gap year-round is 235 to 247 km from Dease Lake to Junction 37, or up to 270 km from Dease Lake into Watson Lake, because Good Hope Lake and Jade City have no reliable retail fuel. South of Dease Lake, Bell II Lodge to Iskut is a 158 km winter gap, and Bell II closes its pumps at 5:30 p.m. in winter. 4. The Dempster and Inuvik-Tuktoyaktuk Highways (Yukon Highway 5, NWT Highway 8, and NWT Highway 10, Dawson City to Tuktoyaktuk): 736 km to Inuvik plus 138 km to the Arctic Ocean at Tuktoyaktuk (874 km total). Longest fuel gap is 369 km on crushed-shale gravel from Dempster Corner (km 0) to Eagle Plains Hotel, followed by 181 km to Fort McPherson, 186 km to Inuvik, and 138 km each way (276 km round-trip) with zero highway services between Inuvik and Tuktoyaktuk. Completely impassable across the Peel and Mackenzie rivers for 6 to 8 weeks during fall freeze-up and 3 to 5 weeks during spring break-up. 5. The Cowboy Trail (Alberta Highway 22, Mayerthorpe to Lundbreck): 584 km on Highway 22 (roughly 700 km full trail). Longest fuel gap is 109.1 km from the southern edge of Longview to the Highway 3 junction at Lundbreck. Neither Bar U Ranch nor Chain Lakes Provincial Park sells gasoline, and 80 to 100 km/h Chinook headwinds off the Livingstone Range burn fuel fast. 6. The Trans-Labrador Highway (NL Route 500 and Route 510) and Lake Superior's North Shore (Ontario Highway 17): 1,149 km across Labrador and 700 km from Sault Ste. Marie to Thunder Bay. Route 510 from Happy Valley-Goose Bay to Port Hope Simpson runs 402 to 406 km with zero highway gas stations, and Route 500 from Churchill Falls to Goose Bay runs 288 km without services. On Ontario Highway 17, Batchawana Bay to Wawa is a 145 to 150 km gap through Lake Superior Provincial Park where the old Agawa Bay stations closed years ago. 1. The Icefields Parkway (Alberta Highway 93 North): 232 km and a locked October gate Every October, someone rents an all-wheel-drive crossover at the Calgary airport, tops up the tank on Macleod Trail, drives past Canmore and Lake Louise without looking at the fuel gauge, and discovers at Bow Summit that the Icefields Parkway is not a suburban parkway with service plazas. Highway 93 North runs 232 kilometres (144 miles) from the Trans-Canada Highway junction near Lake Louise to the Yellowhead Highway junction at Jasper. For roughly 200 of those kilometres, from just north of the Lake Louise tramline all the way to Athabasca Falls, there is zero cellular service. There are no call boxes, no roaming bars on any carrier, and no tow trucks waiting behind a snowbank. The Saskatchewan River Crossing trap: On paper, the Parkway has a gas station at The Crossing Resort at Saskatchewan River Crossing, where Highway 93 North meets the David Thompson Highway (Highway 11), roughly 77 kilometres north of Lake Louise and 153 kilometres south of Jasper. What your mapping app rarely mentions in bold letters is that The Crossing Resort is strictly seasonal. Every year, The Crossing closes its doors, its kitchen, and its gas pumps in mid-October and does not reopen until mid-April or early May. There is no 24-hour winter cardlock at the junction. Neither the Columbia Icefield Glacier Discovery Centre at kilometre 127 (which also closes in mid-October) nor Sunwapta Falls Resort carries public fuel. When The Crossing locks up for the season, the Icefields Parkway becomes a continuous 232-kilometre fuel gap through high alpine passes where cold air, winter-grade gasoline, snow-covered pavement, and mountain grades quietly shave 15 to 20 percent off your highway range. Even in July and August, when The Crossing's pumps are running, every drop of fuel is trucked in and priced accordingly, and the pumps are not open overnight. Winter tires and plow hours: Parks Canada enforces mandatory winter tires (bearing the Three-Peak Mountain Snowflake 3PMSF or M+S symbol) or snow chains on the Icefields Parkway from November 1 to March 31, and at any other time when snow or ice covers the road surface. If you are connecting from Golden or Radium Hot Springs on the British Columbia side, BC's winter tire law (3.5 mm minimum tread depth) kicks in a full month earlier, from October 1 to April 30. The tip: Highway maintenance crews on Highway 93 North only plow and sand between 7:00 a.m. and 3:30 p.m. Outside those hours, the road is not maintained, and avalanche control or whiteouts across Bow Summit (2,088 metres) and Sunwapta Pass (2,035 metres) can close the gates at Lake Louise and Jasper for hours or days at a time.
