The first time you see a bear that is not on a roadside Instagram story, your brain does something unhelpful. It freezes the soundtrack, invents a TED Talk about sprinting, and forgets every poster you ever read at a trailhead. This guide is for that second. Not the vibes. The manners. Western Canada is bear country on purpose. Banff, Jasper, Kananaskis, Yoho, Kootenay, the Chilcotin, the Yukon — black bears and grizzlies share the valleys we hike, bike and camp in. Parks Canada is blunt: you can meet a bear on a busy trail near town or deep in the backcountry. Most encounters end without injury when people stay calm, make space, and treat spray as a last-resort tool, not cologne. This is the practical version: ID, noise, groups, spray, defensive vs non-defensive behaviour, bluff charges, contact, food storage, dogs, bikes and runners. Sourced from Parks Canada mountain-parks guidance, Alberta Parks / Alberta.ca, WildSafeBC and BC Parks as of November 2026. Check local closures before you go. Black bear or grizzly: stop guessing by colour Colour is a trap. Parks Canada is clear: both species come in blond, brown, cinnamon and near-black. A "black" bear can be cinnamon. A grizzly can look blond or almost black. Size is a trap too — a female grizzly and a male black bear in the Rockies can weigh about the same. Use shape, face and claws, the way Parks Canada wildlife staff do: Grizzly (Ursus arctos): prominent shoulder hump; rump lower than the shoulders; large round head with a dished / concave face profile; short, round ears set apart; long curved claws (about 5–10 cm) that often leave marks well ahead of the toes in tracks. Average Rockies weights Parks Canada lists: males roughly 150–300 kg, females 80–150 kg. Black bear (Ursus americanus): no big shoulder hump; rump higher than the shoulders; smaller head with a straighter snout-to-forehead line; longer, pointier ears; short claws (about 3–4 cm) that are excellent for climbing. Chest blazes (white patches) are common. Rockies averages: males about 80–150 kg, females 45–100 kg. You will not always get a perfect side view. Do not edge closer for one. ID from a safe distance, then act on behaviour. Avoid the meeting: noise, groups, signs and hours Parks Canada's first rule is the boring one that saves the most people: avoid the encounter. Make noise that means something. Call out, clap, sing or talk — especially near streams, dense brush, berry patches, on windy days, and anywhere you cannot see ahead. Parks Canada and Alberta Parks both say bear bells are not enough. Bears can be so locked on food they miss quiet humans. Hike tight, hike daylight. Parks Canada recommends a tight group of four or more. Never let kids wander. Travel marked trails in daylight; bears are often more active at dawn and dusk. Read the ground. Fresh tracks, diggings, torn logs, flipped rocks, scat, and especially a carcass mean leave — immediately — and report a dead animal to Parks Canada or the local provincial line. Ravens and magpies hanging around can also mean a carcass. Headphones off. Banff, Jasper and Alberta Parks all say it: tune in; do not recreate with earbuds in. Stay 100 m from bears when you can (Parks Canada frames that as about ten bus lengths). Never approach. Never feed. Roadside bears need space to put on fat for winter — consider not stopping at all. Bear spray: the last-resort tool that still belongs on your belt Carry spray. Know it. Do not romanticize it. Parks Canada describes bear spray as a capsaicin deterrent that throws a cone-shaped cloud roughly 10 m at over 100 km/h. It stings eyes and skin and makes breathing miserable. It is non-lethal. Alberta.ca and WildSafeBC push the same logistics Parks Canada does: Buy spray labelled for animals / bear repellent. In Canada the package cannot exceed 500 ml. Parks Canada recommends about 225 ml with 0.75%–1% capsaicin. Check the expiry date. Propellant weakens. Expired cans are trail jewellery. Carry it where your dominant hand can grab it in under two seconds — hip or chest holster. In a pack is a no. On a bike, Parks Canada suggests the water-bottle cage; Alberta.ca prefers a chest holster or jersey pocket so you still have it if you leave the bike. Remove the transport tie-wrap before you hike. Practice the safety clip at home with an inert trainer if you can. En route: safety on, trunk or sealed container, away from heat and punctures. Not allowed on commercial airlines , even checked. Do not pre-spray tents, gear or yourself. Residual scent can attract bears. When and how: last resort after you try to create space. Parks Canada wants the bear close — less than about a bus length — and Banff officer Dan Rafla, on the Parks Canada spray page, puts effective range around five metres . Safety off, build a wall of spray toward the face, short one- to two-second bursts, respect the wind, then leave. WildSafeBC's S.P.R.A.Y. cue helps under stress: Stop, Prepare, Remove safety, build A wall, Yield. Wind, rain, cold and expiry all cut effectiveness. Avoidance still beats heroics. If you see a bear: aware, unaware, bluff charge Stop. Stay calm. Get spray ready. Do not run. Running can trigger a chase, and you will not win. Unaware of you: leave quietly without getting its attention. Aware of you: speak calmly and firmly so it knows you are human. Back away slowly. Appear big; pick up small kids; stay grouped. Do not drop your pack — it can protect you. If a bear stands and sniffs the air, Parks Canada says it is usually trying to identify you, not starting a fight. Bears may bluff charge — come hard, then veer or stop. Hold your nerve. Screaming and sudden moves can make things worse. If you must continue past, give a wide detour, leave the trail, or wait at a safe distance until it moves on. Defensive vs non-defensive: why the script changes Parks Canada splits approaching bears into two stories. Get the story wrong and the response is wrong. Defensive (most common bad encounter): the bear is surprised, protecting cubs, or guarding food. It looks stressed — woofing, growling, jaw-popping, ears back. Your job is to look non-threatening: calm voice, no hard eye contact staring contest, slowly increase distance when it pauses. If it keeps coming, stand your ground, keep talking, use spray. If it makes contact in a defensive attack, play dead (details below). Non-defensive (curious, food-conditioned, dominance-testing, or — rarely — predatory): head and ears up, intent on you. Speak firmly, get out of its path, stand your ground if followed, shout, look big, intimidate, use spray if it closes. This is not the "look small and apologetic" script. Alberta Parks echoes the same split and notes some sprays list effective distances up to about 9 m depending on brand and wind — always read your can. If contact happens Most encounters never get here. If they do, Parks Canada’s mountain-parks guidance: Defensive attack (most common): use spray. If the bear makes contact, play dead . Lie on your stomach, legs apart, hands crossed behind your neck to protect face and neck and make it harder to flip you. Stay still until you are sure it has left. Defensive assaults are often under about two minutes. If the attack continues, it may have shifted — fight back . Predatory attack (very rare): stalking on a trail, or an attack at night. Do not play dead. Try to reach a building, vehicle or climb a tree if you can. Otherwise spray and fight — shout, hit with rocks or sticks, make it clear you are not easy prey.
