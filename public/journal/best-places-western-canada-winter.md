Ten places in Alberta and BC that save their best for winter. Most people treat the first real snowfall like a door closing. Patio's done, hikes are done, see you in May. Those people are missing the best show of the year. Some places in Western Canada are fine in summer and unreal in winter. Waterfalls stop mid-fall and hang there as blue ice. A lake in the foothills locks thousands of white bubbles inside glass. The crowds at Lake Louise thin out, the lake turns into a skating rink, and the glacier at the far end looks close enough to touch. The best places to visit in Western Canada in winter aren't the ones you'd guess from a postcard. Here are our ten, with when to go, how to get there and what to wear. We checked closures, road rules and safety notes against Parks Canada, BC Parks, Alberta Health Services and local operators in November 2026. Two famous names are closed or limited this season, and we'll say which. Check again before you go. 1. Lake Louise, Banff National Park, AB In July you're shoulder to shoulder with a tour bus for a photo of a turquoise lake. In January the lake is white, the glacier glows pink at sunrise, and you can skate across the same water. Good trade. Skating usually starts around mid-December, once the ice is thick enough for the daily snow clearing, and runs into spring. It's free to skate; you just need a national park pass. The Fairmont Chateau Lake Louise typically builds an ice castle beside the rink, and the lake is lit for evening skating. If you don't skate, walk the lakeshore trail. Parks Canada lists it at 2.3 km one way. Turn around at the end of the lake, where you can see frozen Louise Falls: past that point is avalanche terrain, and it's for people with training and gear. Ice Magic, the ice sculpture event, runs February 3 to 7, 2027. It changed this year. There are no general admission tickets: you get in by staying at a participating Lake Louise hotel (or by being a local resident on two set days), and everyone rides a shuttle, because there's no parking at the site. When: mid-December to March for skating and snow-covered ice. November is scenery only; the lake may still be open water. Getting there: less than an hour west of Banff on Highway 1, then up Lake Louise Drive. Paid lakeshore parking only runs during the summer shuttle season (May 15 to October 12 in 2026), so winter parking is free. The lots still fill on weekends, so go early. What to wear: skating is cold work for your ears. Johnston Canyon Icewalk, Banff National Park, AB This is the walk that ruins every other winter walk. Steel catwalks bolted to the canyon walls carry you over Johnston Creek, which is still moving under a skin of ice, while the waterfalls freeze in place. The Upper Falls, about 30 m tall, turns into a wall of blue columns that look poured rather than grown. Parks Canada lists the Lower Falls at 1.2 km one way with about 50 m of climbing, and the Upper Falls at 2.5 km one way with about 120 m. It's easy walking on paper. On ice, it's a different sport. Parks Canada calls the trail "usually very icy" and strongly recommends ice cleats. You can buy them at the Banff Visitor Centre at 224 Banff Avenue and at most outdoor shops in town. Every winter there's a guy in running shoes sliding down the stairs on both hands. Don't be that guy. Heads up for this season: Parks Canada closed the Johnston Canyon trail, the day-use area, the washrooms and the main P1 parking lot on October 13, 2026 for rock-slope work. The closure order ends December 11, 2026. Check the Banff bulletins before you drive out. During the closure, the Upper Falls and the Ink Pots can still be reached the long way from the Moose Meadows trailhead, which has limited parking. When: from mid-December, once the trail reopens, through March. Weekday mornings are quietest; it's one of the busiest winter walks in the park. Getting there: the Bow Valley Parkway (Highway 1A) between Banff and Lake Louise. A national park pass is required. What to wear: cleats first, then layers. You'll warm up on the climb and cool off fast at the falls, so a zip layer you can open and close helps. Abraham Lake Ice Bubbles, David Thompson Country, AB You've seen the photo even if you didn't know where it was taken: white discs stacked under black-green ice, a mountain glowing behind. That's Abraham Lake, west of Nordegg on Highway 11, and on a good day it really looks like that. Abraham Lake is Alberta's largest reservoir, made in 1972 when the Bighorn Dam went in on the North Saskatchewan River. Plants and trees on the lake bottom rot and release methane. The gas rises, the lake freezes over it, and the next batch gets trapped under that, layer after layer, until you get stacks. Wind scours the snow off and leaves the ice clear enough to look straight down into. Ice safety, for real: this is wild ice on a reservoir, not a rink. Alberta Health Services says ice should be at least 15 cm thick before you walk on it, and that's for clear, solid ice. Local guides at Nordegg Adventures say to stay off the ice between the dam and Windy Point, where water levels swing, to avoid spots where creeks and the river come in, and to stay off snow-covered patches that can hide weak ice. Grey or slushy ice is a no. Check thickness as you go, not just once at the shore. Wear cleats, bring ice picks, don't go alone, and expect limited cell service. If you can't judge ice, hire a guide or stay on shore. The bubbles look nearly as good from the edge. When: Preacher's Point, at the south end where the river comes in, usually freezes first, often by mid-November, with fine bubbles and shallow ice. For the deep stacks over turquoise water, local guides say January 10 to 25 is usually a safe bet. Fresh snow hides everything, so watch the forecast. Getting there: Highway 11 west of Nordegg; it also links to the Icefields Parkway at Saskatchewan River Crossing. Fill up in Nordegg and carry a winter road kit. What to wear: Abraham Lake has a reputation for wind, and wind is what keeps the ice clear, so it's coming whether you like it or not. The Icefields Parkway, Banff and Jasper National Parks, AB The Icefields Parkway is 232 km of road between Lake Louise and Jasper, and it's on every list of the best drives on Earth for good reason. In summer you share it with a few thousand rental RVs. In winter you might go twenty minutes without another car, with mountain goats on the cliffs, frozen waterfalls on the rock faces and lakes gone white. Good stops, south to north: Bow Lake (34 km from Lake Louise), Peyto Lake (40 km), the Weeping Wall, which turns into a giant ice climbing wall in winter (106 km), and Athabasca Falls (32 km from Jasper). Parks Canada also flags a 20 km stretch that runs through winter caribou habitat, home to the Jasper herd of woodland caribou. Drive the speed limit there. Not every pullout is plowed, so if a lot isn't cleared, keep going rather than parking on the shoulder. This road is serious in winter. There are no services between Lake Louise and Jasper: no gas, no food, no hotels, and no cell coverage. Snow tires (snowflake or M+S symbol) or chains are mandatory from November 1 to March 31.
